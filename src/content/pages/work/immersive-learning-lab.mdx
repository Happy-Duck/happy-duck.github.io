## Why a headset

Real scientific instruments and institutions are often out of the public's reach, both physically and in how hard they are to understand. Space science is a good case for showing them in 3D instead. Its instruments fly on missions still in development, its physics happens in three dimensions that a flat diagram conveys poorly, and its research centers are on the other side of the world.

VR helps on each count. It runs on consumer hardware, the Meta Quest, that researchers, students, and the public can get hold of. It lets a visitor look at an instrument from the inside, and scale it up to see how a compact system fits together. And it uses the sense of space people already have, which a flat picture does not.

The app has two halves: a replica of a real building in Bern, and a model of an instrument built there for a comet mission.

## The lobby in Bern

The International Space Science Institute (ISSI) in Bern is a neutral institute for space and Earth science, working across planetary science, astrophysics, cosmology, the earth sciences, astrobiology, and more. Its main-floor lobby is where about a thousand visiting scientists a year meet. The app has a nearly photorealistic replica of it.

The things in the room run on physics, can be handled, and put themselves back afterwards:

- coffee mugs that fill, and can be drunk from or spilled, and food that can be eaten
- the ISSI bell, whose clapper swings on a physics joint
- the institute's published books, and other odds and ends

Facts about the institute are posted around the room, from how much it publishes to how much coffee it gets through. Non-player "scientists" wander the space, and a mural carries a menu that links to the lab's other experiences.

## The instrument

MANiaC, the Mass Analyzer for Neutrals in a Coma, is a mass spectrometer from the University of Bern, to be flown on ESA's Comet Interceptor mission (2028 to 2029). The app shows it three ways, as a schematic, a realistic model, and a transparent cross-section, in a room made to look like a space station.

It simulates particles moving through the instrument and the time each one takes to reach the detector. Every arrival is plotted as it happens, time of flight against mass per charge, which is how the instrument tells one ion species from another.

<Figure src="/media/work/immersive-learning-lab/maniac-plot.webp" alt="A graph of mass per charge against flight time in nanoseconds: blue dots low on the left, yellow dots rising to the right" caption="The live plot. Each dot is one particle reaching the detector." />

To make that stick, the learner goes along for the ride: they travel through the instrument's chambers as particles of different masses and charges.

## How it was built

- Unity, with OpenXR and the XR Interaction Toolkit, deployed to the Meta Quest
- 3D assets modeled in Blender and textured in Substance Painter
- Tuned for the Quest's mobile hardware: URP Forward+ with baked lighting, level-of-detail groups, and fixed foveated rendering
- Physics-driven interactions on Unity's Rigidbody and Joint systems, with custom scripts for proximity triggers and for putting objects back
- XCharts for the graphs, and AI Navigation for the wandering scientists

Next, as of the poster in April 2026: science challenges and story elements in the lobby for each of ISSI's four disciplines, and a view of a comet's coma for MANiaC.
